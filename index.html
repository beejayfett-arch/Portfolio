Approach

Operations work that moves a number, or it didn't matter.

01

Decompose, don't decorate.

Broad statuses hide operational reality. "Attempting contact" can mean a fresh lead, 17 dial attempts, or a dead deal. I break stages down until each one maps to a real next action, owner, and SLA, then build the reporting on top of that.

02

Definitions before dashboards.

A dashboard is only as good as the entry/exit logic feeding it. I standardise lifecycle definitions across sales, marketing and ops before a single chart goes on a deck. Causal, owned, bounded, that's the test before a metric earns a tile.

03

Automate the repeatable, not the judgement.

AI and workflow automation are operational leverage, not magic. I deploy them on the repetitive parts of the funnel, qualification, follow-up, enrichment, retrieval, so humans spend their hours on the calls and decisions that actually convert.

04

Build for the next 10x, not the last quarter.

Most operational pain comes from systems designed for a smaller business than the one they're now serving. I diagnose structural inefficiency before it becomes a scaling problem, and rebuild architecture so the next wave of growth doesn't break it again.

01 · Funnel & Lifecycle Architecture

Rebuilding the national lead funnel for a scaling buyers' agency.

Aussie · Buyers Agent operations · ~1,000 referrals/month, 30+ BAs, national, multi-source

Scale

  • ~1,000 inbound referrals/month flowing through the funnel
  • Network scaled from ~13 to 30+ buyers' agents nationally
  • Multi-source acquisition: referral partners, paid, organic, internal
  • National operations footprint across states, with state-level reporting

Context

The buyers' agent business was scaling fast, referral volume climbing, headcount more than doubling, lead sources multiplying. The CRM funnel hadn't kept pace. Every team was using the same stage labels to describe completely different operational states.

Problem

A lead sitting in "Attempting Contact" for three days and 17 dial attempts looked identical to a lead called once, identical to a lead nobody had picked up at all. Reporting was directionally useless. Reps had no shared definition of what "good follow-up" looked like. Leadership couldn't see where leads were actually dying.

Diagnosis

The root cause wasn't behaviour, it was architecture. The lifecycle model gave us no granularity to act on. Coaching, SLAs, automation, and attribution all needed operationally distinct states to hang off. Without that, every downstream system - from reporting to nurture, was guessing.

What I built

  • Decomposed "Attempting Contact" into Attempt 1 → Attempt 5, each with entry/exit logic, expected cadence, and a defined next action.
  • Reworked stage progression so movement happened on operational events (call logged, SMS sent, voicemail left), not rep opinion.
  • Mapped ownership across Sales, Admin and BA teams, every stage had one accountable role.
  • Built callback sequencing logic and aging views so overdue follow-ups surfaced before they went cold.
  • Defined an escalation/nurture path for stage-5 leads with no engagement, instead of a silent close-lost.
  • Rebuilt reporting around contactability %, time-to-first-contact, fallout by stage, stage velocity, lead aging, not raw lead counts.

Operational thinking

Stages aren't reporting categories, they're the unit of accountability. I want every stage to answer: who owns this lead right now, what's the next action, and when is it overdue? If a stage can't answer those three questions, it's not a stage, it's noise.

Outcome

  • Funnel reporting became trusted at leadership level for the first time.
  • Time-to-first-contact and contactability % became coachable, owned metrics.
  • Stage-level fallout analysis became possible, pinpointing where to invest in automation, scripts, or headcount.
  • Stale lead volume dropped; overdue follow-ups stopped slipping through.
  • Operational adherence improved because overdue work became visible by architecture, not management intervention.
  • Accountability rose without adding management overhead, the system did the surfacing.

What I'd do differently

I'd build the attribution layer in parallel, not after. We had operational granularity on contact attempts before we had clean lead-source dedup, which meant we could describe how leads moved but not always why one source's leads moved better. In a HubSpot-native stack I'd own UTM taxonomy and source/dedup logic in the same sprint as the lifecycle redesign.

02 · Executive Reporting & Attribution

Making the funnel legible to leadership.

Aussie · ELT / GLT operating cadence · Salesforce reporting stack

Reporting surface

  • Weekly ops cadence, funnel throughput, contactability, callback adherence
  • Monthly leadership reporting, pipeline, conversion, source quality, state variance
  • Quarterly strategic reviews, referral economics, capacity, intervention priorities
  • Cross-functional alignment across sales, marketing, ops and finance on shared definitions

Context

Leadership reviews were pulling numbers from five different definitions of the same metric. "Contacted" meant one thing in a sales deck, something else in a marketing report, and something else again in a state-level operations review.

Problem

Without shared definitions, every metric on every dashboard had to be re-litigated before a decision could be made. The reporting layer was eating executive attention and producing low-conviction calls.

Diagnosis

The issue wasn't tooling, it was that we had no governance over what each lifecycle metric actually meant. Different teams had built reports against different field states, and nobody owned the source-of-truth definition.

What I built

  • A standardised KPI dictionary with entry/exit criteria for every lifecycle and conversion event.
  • Executive dashboards built around bottlenecks: leakage points, stage velocity, conversion lag, source-quality decay.
  • Reporting cadence tied to the operating rhythm, weekly ops, monthly GLT, quarterly ELT, with each view answering one decision.
  • Operational hygiene rules feeding the dashboards: dedup, source integrity, record completeness, ownership.
  • A "metric goes on a dashboard only if it's causal, owned, and bounded" rule that killed about 40% of legacy tiles.

Operational thinking

A dashboard isn't a deliverable, it's a decision surface. If a chart doesn't change what someone does next week, it's vanity. I'd rather ship four trusted tiles than forty contested ones.

Outcome

  • Leadership discussions shifted from debating definitions to debating interventions.
  • State and team-level performance became comparable on the same axes.
  • Source-quality decay, previously invisible, became a tracked, owned metric.
  • State-level conversion variance was exposed, enabling targeted coaching and resource shifts.
  • Lead aging surfaced as a leading indicator instead of a lagging post-mortem.
  • Funnel leakage isolated by stage, investment decisions became defensible.

Artifact, the dashboard in production

What this dashboard demonstrates in one frame:

  • The lifecycle decomposition from Case 01 in production. The pre-engagement status donut shows the actual operational stages (New Lead, Attempting Contact, Contacted and In Progress, Nurture, Property Strategy Session, Customer Agreement) rather than the broad "contacted / not contacted" view it replaced.
  • Volume scale. Monthly referral intake scaled from ~12 in late 2024 to ~1K/month in 2026, the system was load-tested by growth, not just designed for it.
  • Causal, owned, bounded. Every tile answers one question with one definition. % conversion is reported against a single lifecycle transition, not five interpretations of "converted".
  • Leadership-grade roll-up. All-time, quarter, status mix, monthly trend, and conversion search date are visible on one screen, the operating cadence sits on this surface.

What I'd do differently

Earlier in the project I treated definitions as a documentation task. They're really a change-management task. Next time I'd run the dictionary as a stakeholder workshop with reps, managers and finance in the same room, so the definitions ship with buy-in instead of needing it retrofitted.

03 · AI-driven Operational Leverage

2-way AI SMS for qualification & booking, plus retrieval for proposals.

Wonder Group · MyCRMSIM · Go High Level · ZappyChat · OpenAI

Context

Two operational chokepoints kept reappearing across the brands: inbound enquiries that went cold while reps were on calls, and proposal/case-study assembly that ate hours per deal. Both were repetitive, language-heavy, and high-volume, classic AI surface area.

Problem

Response latency on inbound SMS was killing conversion on the easiest, hottest leads. And every customised proposal pulled the same handful of case studies, testimonials and credentials out of scattered drives by hand.

Diagnosis

Neither problem needed "more AI", they needed AI placed precisely against a workflow with clear inputs and outputs. The qualification SMS bot had to book, not chat. The proposal retrieval tool had to cite, not invent.

What I built

  • 2-way AI SMS qualifier: Android device pool → MyCRMSIM → Go High Level → ZappyChat → OpenAI, with handoff back to a human rep at booking intent or when confidence dropped.
  • Lifecycle-aware prompting: the bot's tone and questions changed based on the lead's stage, source and prior touchpoints, not a single system prompt.
  • AI search & retrieval for proposals: indexed past case studies, testimonials and outcomes; rep types a brief, gets a draft with sourced excerpts.
  • Guardrails: hard-coded escalation triggers (complaints, contract questions, anything price-binding) and conversation logging back to CRM for audit.
  • Measurement: instrumented the bot against time-to-first-response, qualification rate, booking rate and human-takeover rate, not just "messages sent".

Operational thinking

I don't deploy AI against a job title, I deploy it against a workflow with a measurable edge. The SMS bot exists because seconds-to-response is a conversion lever I can move. The retrieval tool exists because proposal cycle time is a deal velocity lever. Frame the AI work commercially, or it stalls at "cool demo".

The system wasn't designed to replace reps. It was designed to preserve speed during peak inbound periods without linear headcount growth.

Why this mattered commercially

  • Inbound response latency reduced from hours to seconds, the easiest, hottest leads stopped going cold.
  • Recovered pipeline previously lost to delayed engagement.
  • Manual handling load on reps reduced, they spent their hours on calls that actually converted.
  • Proposal assembly time dropped meaningfully via retrieval, lifting deal velocity.
  • Headcount stayed flat as inbound volume rose, operational leverage in the truest sense.
  • Conversation logs became a coaching corpus, actual customer language, captured at scale.

What I'd do differently

I built the SMS bot before the evaluation harness. That meant the first month of tuning was vibes, not numbers. Today I'd start with a small labelled set of real inbound messages and an offline eval before the first message ever goes to a customer. Same for retrieval: build the eval set first, then optimise the prompt and chunking against it.

04 · Multi-Brand RevOps

Unifying three brands without flattening them.

Wonder Group · Wedded Wonderland · Liviti · St Trinity

Shape of the problem

  • 3 brands, 3 motions: B2B marketplace, residential property sales, enablement / media
  • B2B and B2C journeys sharing back-office systems
  • Multiple customer types, vendors, agents, buyers, partners
  • Fragmented attribution and inconsistent onboarding across the group
  • Duplicate lifecycle logic appearing under different stage names per brand

Context

Three brands, three sales motions, three flavours of customer journey, one increasingly tangled shared operations layer. Property on one side, B2B marketplace and media on the other, and an enablement business in between.

Problem

Lead handling, lifecycle stages, onboarding and reporting had diverged brand-by-brand. Customers touching more than one brand got inconsistent experiences. Leadership couldn't roll up performance without manual reconciliation.

Diagnosis

The fix wasn't "one system to rule them all", the brands had genuinely different motions. What they shared was the operational shape: capture → qualify → engage → convert → onboard. Standardise the shape, let the content vary.

What I built

  • A shared lifecycle skeleton across brands, with brand-specific sub-stages where the motion genuinely diverged.
  • Standardised lead capture and source taxonomy so cross-brand attribution was possible.
  • Onboarding workflows aligned around handoff events, not calendar dates.
  • Documented operational playbooks per brand sitting on top of the shared skeleton.
  • A roll-up reporting layer that finally let leadership compare brand-to-brand without re-keying data.

Operational thinking

The challenge wasn't standardising brands. It was standardising operational shape while preserving different sales motions. Multi-brand operations fail when you force one motion onto every brand, and they fail when you let every brand reinvent the wheel. The trick is to find the thinnest shared layer that gives you roll-up reporting and cross-brand mobility, then leave the brand-specific work where it belongs.

Outcome

  • Cross-brand leakage and overlap became visible for the first time.
  • Sales and marketing handoffs stopped failing at the brand boundary.
  • Onboarding consistency improved across the group.
  • Reporting could finally answer "how is the group doing?" without three spreadsheets.

What I'd do differently

I'd push harder, earlier, on a single source-of-truth for contact and account records across brands. We made the lifecycle shape consistent before we fully solved the identity layer, which left some attribution edges noisier than they needed to be.

05 · CRM & Data Architecture

Redesigning CRM structure for entities the system was never built for.

Zoho CRM redesign · trusts, SMSFs, corporate entities, contract lifecycles

Context

The CRM had been set up around individual buyers. The actual business was increasingly transacting with trusts, SMSFs and corporate entities, each with their own contract timelines and reporting needs.

Problem

  • Settlement tracking living in spreadsheets, invisible to reporting and automation
  • Duplicate contact and entity records created to fake missing relationships
  • Fragmented ownership visibility across trust groups and corporate accounts
  • Reporting blind spots around trusts/SMSFs, couldn't answer "how many active contracts under this trust?"
  • Operational inefficiency compounding with every new deal

Diagnosis

This was an entity model problem, not a process problem. The CRM lacked the object structure to represent the real-world relationships, so users were faking it with duplicate records and freetext fields.

Users were compensating for schema limitations operationally. Duplicate entry and shadow spreadsheets were symptoms, not the root problem. The downstream pain, reporting, automation, settlement tracking, all traced back upstream to the data model.

What I built (and proposed)

  • An entity restructure introducing proper objects for trusts, SMSFs and corporate accounts, with relationships back to individuals.
  • Contract lifecycle tracking with dated milestones (signed, cooling-off, settlement, anniversary) as first-class fields.
  • Settlement task automation off contract date fields, no more diaried reminders.
  • Reporting structures that could finally answer entity-level and contract-lifecycle questions.

Operational thinking

I want to spot structural inefficiency before it becomes a scaling problem. If users are working around the data model, duplicate records, sprawling freetext, shadow spreadsheets, that's a load-bearing signal. The fix is upstream, in the schema, not downstream in more training.

Outcome

  • Entity-level reporting became possible for the first time.
  • Settlement-related tasks moved from manual to automated.
  • Duplicate data entry dropped, and contract-lifecycle visibility was no longer dependent on whoever owned the spreadsheet.

What I'd do differently

I'd separate the schema migration from the workflow rebuild and ship them as two phases instead of one. Bundling them meant users were learning new objects and new processes at the same time. Migrate first, stabilise, then rebuild on top.

06 · QA, Compliance & GTM Enablement

Operational controls that scale with the team, not against it.

Aussie · QA framework, compliance review, product rollout enablement

Scale & exposure

  • Operations scaling across multiple states and a growing operator base
  • Growing operational inconsistency file-to-file, operator-to-operator
  • State-level compliance exposure rising with volume
  • Customer experience variability at brand-damaging scale
  • No recurring issue visibility, failure modes weren't captured anywhere

Context

Rapid growth meant different operators were handling files differently. Compliance exposure was rising, customer experience was inconsistent, and recurring failure modes weren't being captured anywhere.

Problem

At scale, inconsistency compounds quietly until it becomes operational drag or regulatory risk. There was no structured way to see where things were breaking, only that they occasionally did, usually after the fact.

What I built

  • Operational review checkpoints on live files, with standardised audit criteria.
  • Escalation pathways for recurring failure patterns, owned by named roles.
  • Issue tracking that fed back into training and process, not just file-level fixes.
  • For GTM rollouts: operational readiness workflows, frontline adoption support, and feedback loops that surfaced where the new process was breaking before it became a customer issue.

Outcome

  • Compliance failures and customer experience defects became visible, owned and trending.
  • Product/process rollouts landed with shorter adoption tails and earlier issue detection.
07 · Lean Process Re-Architecture

Rebuilding the talent acquisition flow for throughput.

Zoho Desk / Zoho Recruit · hiring manager to onboarded hire · approvals, sourcing, interview, offer, onboarding

Shape of the problem

  • High cycle time from role approval to hire
  • Multiple redundant approval points spread across the flow
  • Sourcing restarting unnecessarily after late-stage rejection
  • Late-stage salary misalignment killing offers at the finish line
  • Inconsistent candidate progression between roles and recruiters
  • Poor visibility into pipeline health at any given moment
  • Excessive operational handoffs between hiring manager, TA, finance, ops

Context

Hiring throughput was constrained by a fragmented, multi-step process with excessive approval loops, inconsistent sequencing, and repeated rework cycles. As headcount plans grew, the process was bending under load that the original design never anticipated.

Problem

Every role started clean and ended messy. Candidates progressed before budget certainty, sourcing channels weren't gated against approval, and the same conditional loops fired repeatedly. The TA function looked busy but the cycle time told a different story.

Diagnosis

The issue wasn't hiring capability, it was flow design. The process contained too many conditional loops and late-stage decision points, so work was repeatedly invalidated instead of progressing forward.

The system optimised for control, not flow. As volume increased, that tradeoff created latency, rework, and inconsistent outcomes. Every additional approval point looked like risk reduction in isolation, and added up to a process that couldn't scale.

Before / After

Click either diagram to open full size. The same boxes, sequenced for flow instead of control.

What I changed

01
Moved salary and finance approval upstream

Before: candidates progressed before budget certainty, so offers blew up at negotiation.

After: salary banded and finance approved before sourcing was greenlit.

Impact: reduced late-stage rejection, eliminated wasted candidate effort, increased offer acceptance confidence.

02
Simplified sourcing pathways

Before: sourcing entry points were unclear and disconnected, hard to attribute or compare.

After: structured channels (post role, headhunt, referrals) running from a single sourcing brief.

Impact: clearer funnel entry, better pipeline visibility, easier tracking of source performance per role and per recruiter.

03
Introduced structured interview gating

Before: inconsistent progression between candidates, depending on who was driving.

After: defined gates, 1-way video, panel interview, recruiter document collection, each with an explicit approval point.

Impact: reduced ambiguity, improved candidate filtering, clearer decision ownership at every stage.

04
Reduced rework loops

Before: reject paths fed straight back into "restart sourcing", repeatedly.

After: upstream filtering caught the rejections that didn't need to happen downstream; the remaining restart paths were deliberate, not default.

Impact: improved cycle efficiency, reduced wasted effort, improved throughput per recruiter.

05
Aligned onboarding and post-hire review

Before: hire and onboarding were a handoff cliff. Post-hire performance disappeared from the process.

After: candidate onboarding, hardware and account setup, and a 3-month performance review tied back into the TA cycle as a closing approval, with explicit "TA role complete" or "restart sourcing" outcomes.

Impact: the process now learns from its own outputs instead of dropping them.

Operational thinking

Every step in a process is either moving the candidate forward or asking permission to keep going. Most broken processes are dense with the second kind. I rebuild by asking two questions: which approvals can move upstream so they gate the work instead of interrupting it? and which decision points are doing real filtering versus performing control? What's left is the flow.

Outcome

  • Cycle time from requisition to hire materially reduced.
  • Late-stage offer collapse on salary all but disappeared.
  • Sourcing channel performance became visible per role and per recruiter.
  • Pipeline health at any week became answerable in one view, not three spreadsheets.
  • Onboarding and 3-month review fed back into the TA function as a closing signal, not an orphan step.

What I'd do differently

I'd version the SLAs against each gate from day one. The flow design held, but for the first quarter we were debating "what counts as overdue" at each step. Bake those SLAs into the redesign and you get the throughput conversation for free instead of paying for it later.
